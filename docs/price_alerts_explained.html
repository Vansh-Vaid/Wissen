Price alerts, explained simply

Two kinds of rule, one idea: tell me on the day something crosses a line.

Imagine drawing a line on a price chart. The tool watches the price day by day and shouts “alert!” only on the day the price jumps from one side of the line to the other. Not before, not after, not while it stays on the new side.

Rule 1: the level rule

{"id": "r1", "instrument": "INFY", "type": "level", "direction": "below", "level": 1500}

In plain words: “Tell me the day INFY drops below ₹1500.” The line is a fixed price, 1500.

INFY closing pricethe level (1500)alert day

This chart was drawn from real numbers run through the tool. The below 1500 rule fires twice:

  • Sep 3: Sep 2 was 1534.10 (above the line), Sep 3 is 1498.00 (below). It crossed, so alert. This is the brief's own example.
  • Sep 4: still below, but it was already below. No new alert.
  • Sep 7: exactly 1500.00, sitting on the line. Not below, so no alert.
  • Sep 8: 1495, coming down from the line. That counts as crossing, so alert.

An above rule is the mirror image. On this same chart, “above 1500” would fire on Sep 5 (1490 → 1503) and Sep 9 (1495 → 1508).

The four situations to remember

In each small chart the dashed line is the level, 1500, and the rule is “below 1500”.

How the tool does it: for each day it works out close − level.
DayCloseclose − 1500Side
Sep 11520.50+20.50above

Positive means above the line, negative means below, zero means exactly on it. An alert is just “the sign changed since yesterday”.

Rule 2: the moving-average rule

{"id": "r2", "instrument": "TCS", "type": "ma_cross", "short": 5, "long": 20, "direction": "above"}

In plain words: “Tell me the day TCS’s short-term trend rises above its long-term trend.”

What is a moving average?

It is just the average of the last few days. Every day the window slides forward by one day, so the average “moves”.

  • A 5-day average reacts fast: it mostly reflects this week.
  • A 20-day average reacts slowly: it reflects the last month.

When the fast one climbs above the slow one, prices have recently been higher than usual, which many traders read as the trend turning up.

The crossing

Now the “line” is not fixed. It is the slow average, and it moves too. The alert fires on the day the fast line crosses the slow line. This chart is the brief’s TCS example (sample 01), drawn from the real data:

TCS close5-day average (fast)20-day average (slow)alert day

From August the price drifts down, so the fast average stays under the slow one. In mid-September the price turns up. The fast average catches up and, on 24 September, rises above the slow one: alert, exactly as in the brief. (On 23 September they were almost equal, 3911.0 against 3911.06, but not crossed yet.)

Why the first alert can only come on day 21

A 20-day average needs 20 days of prices before it exists. And a crossing needs a yesterday to compare with. So with 5/20, day 21 is the earliest possible alert. A stock with only 20 rows of data can never fire this rule.

Two small tricks in the code
  • No dividing. Instead of comparing sum5 ÷ 5 with sum20 ÷ 20, the tool compares sum5 × 20 with sum20 × 5. That is the same question (multiply both sides by 100), with no rounding.
  • Exact numbers. Prices are stored as Decimal, not normal computer decimals (floats). With floats, 1854.05 + 1497.56 + 1275.19 + 2076.42 can come out as 6703.219999…, which makes two equal averages look different and causes a false alert.

Quick self-test

Level 1500, below. Closes: 1510, 1500, 1499. Which day fires?

Day 3. Day 2 is exactly on the line (not below). Day 3 goes from “on the line” to “below”, which counts.

Level 1500, below. Closes: 1490, 1480, 1470. Which day fires?

None. It starts below and never crosses. Day 1 has no yesterday.

A 5/20 rule on a stock with exactly 20 days of data. Can it fire?

No. The 20-day average exists only on day 20, and day 20 has no yesterday with an average.

Why doesn’t the alert repeat every day the price stays below?

Because the rule is about the moment of crossing, the day the side changes. Staying on the same side is not a change.

Fast and slow averages are exactly equal today, and the fast one is higher tomorrow. When is the alert?

Tomorrow. Equal is not “above”, so today does not fire. Tomorrow it goes from equal to above, so that is the crossing.
